§ Preamble

Where we stand and why we're here

"The only principle that does not inhibit progress is: anything goes."
—Paul Feyerabend, Against Method (3rd ed., 1993), p. 14
In the 1987 preface, also included in this edition (p. vii), Feyerabend says that, following Lakatos, who loved jokes and irony, he occasionally wrote ironically, and as an example he points to the same formula as it stands at the end of chapter 1 (p. 19): "anything goes" is not a "principle" he holds (he doesn't think that principles can be used and fruitfully discussed outside the concrete research situation they are supposed to affect) but the terrified exclamation of a rationalist who takes a closer look at history.

Why

Research done by people satisfying their own curiosity. There's no preoccupation with contributing to science, with titles or with a career. Asking questions and finding honest answers.

How

Principles, not rules. Each investigation finds its own way. There's no fixed methodological recipe. The guiding principles were distilled out of mistakes.

Limits

The best achievable result is not the best possible result. "There is always someone better than you." We'll find the stopping point by intuition.

Measure

Research is measured by what it adds to a person, not by academic standards. But it draws inspiration from the academy's demand for originality and critical distance.

Fig. 1 · Distribution of researchers. A researcher's makeup sets the lower limit of depth.

The quality of the researcher sets the standard of the research. Everyone satisfies their curiosity in a different way. Some are content with a brief look, while others can't rest until they've looked at the subject from the inside. This distinction isn't trivial, because the makeup of the person doing the research sets the lower limit of the research's depth. What decides is makeup, not tools, time or resources.

The answers different people give to the same question can be separated by a chasm in quality. You cannot know the quality of the research without knowing the quality of the researcher.

The principles in this document come from one place, namely mistakes. Each study that was produced over the years and that we later returned to with the question "why didn't this work?" taught something. Behind every principle written in this document, a mistake stands in silence.

§ Founding Principle

Call to the Mind

Research is an invitation to think, not an instruction.

This is what this constitution is for. It's the principle that stands above all the others and defines why they exist. Research does not proclaim definitive conclusions; it offers material for thought. Instead of telling readers what to think, it sends them an invitation to think.

Research that makes a call to the mind:

  • Feels the need to say why it says what it says, and why it makes the kind of call it makes.
  • Knows to expect questions, because a call invites questioning.
  • Knows it's inside a process of thinking, not at a conclusion.
  • Keeps the interaction open. The document is an open invitation, not a closed product.

Being caught in the impasse of certainty means that research stops being a call and turns into an instruction. A text that says "this is definitely so" calls for acceptance, not for thought. Research does not do that.

Every principle (from primary source priority to the verification chain, from drawing maps to anti-performance) serves this purpose: to make an honest, auditable and open call to the reader's mind.

The formulation paradox. Doesn't formulating the principles turn them into instructions? Doesn't a principle that can be stated stop being a lived principle? No, because the Living Method carries the paradox. The principles are formulated, but their application differs every time. A formulation is a map, not the territory. Knowing that the map is not the territory itself makes the map honest, not useless.

"How a conclusion was reached must be visible, so that it can be audited."

All the principles of this constitution rest on the practice of keeping every step of the research process (its success, its failure and its change) traceable and accessible.

Research Without Traps Is Dangerous

"People who don't fall into traps make the consequences grave to live through." Traps belong to the nature of research; research without traps is sterile and dangerously self-confident. A researcher who thinks they're moving forward without making mistakes becomes unable to see their mistakes. Trap = a learning opportunity. A researcher who knows this is afraid of the absence of traps, not of traps.

Behavioral Effect Test of the Preamble Sentence

Test period. It was added in v0.9 (Session 7, 28 March), and 3 sessions have passed since (8-9-10). Result. Partly effective. Discipline increased on the surface. The v1.0 review was thorough, and the memory scan was systematic. But the structural blind spots went unseen. Failing for 8 sessions to detect that training data is unreliable fell short of the depth that the preamble sentence's "invitation to think" had promised. The preamble sentence changed behavior but could not guarantee depth of thought. This is an expected result. A principle changes behavior, but it does not instill a reflex. A reflex is acquired by living it.

§ Framework Principle

Living Method

Each investigation finds its own way.

There's no universal methodological recipe. The principles are fixed, but how they're applied differs every time. Research rests on principles, not on recipes.

Research has a quality that is, in fact, a quality of everything in life. If a person can get inside it and live it, the research starts talking to that person. If you get inside it, you begin to live it, and if you begin to live it, the research looks back at you.

This work moves forward like an orchestra. The human is curious, steers by intuition and corrects, while the AI scans, compiles and questions. It is mutual steering, not a hierarchy of conductor and musicians. The principles themselves were born of this orchestra too.

The principles are a compass, not a gate. A gate is pass or fail, binary and mechanical. A compass shows direction at every step. It's continuous and requires judgment. The moment of gathering information (every time information comes in from outside) switches the compass on automatically. A classification ("this is not research, it's a decision question") cannot switch the compass off.

Written rule ≠ living reflex [v1.2]. It was confirmed in 5 cases (April 2026) that writing a pointer doesn't produce a reflex. Writing a principle into a file doesn't turn it into action. The distance between principle and reflex closes only through lived error. This is the Living Method's hardest test. Even a living method can stay on paper.

The name of the gap: reactive compliance [v1.3]. A month-long review (May and June 2026) showed this gap more sharply. In most places the principles came into play not on their own but when questioned. One session's raw record says bluntly, "you meet the demands of the library principle whenever I question this." The principle was written down and known; what triggered it, though, came from outside. The name of the reflex gap is reactive compliance. A principle that wakes only to a question from outside, not from within, is not yet a reflex.

The solution lies in mechanism, not in reflex [v1.3]. If a principle requires "remembering" again and again, the problem lies in placement, not in memory. What's needed is to move the principle somewhere that requires no reflex, not to write it more often. First comes automation (which requires no remembering at all), failing that a hook (which fires on its own at the moment of action), and failing that an index (queried from a single source of truth). The best learning is the kind that makes itself unnecessary. It embeds the principle in how the system works instead of entrusting it to human reflex. The only known structural answer to the "Written rule ≠ living reflex" test is this: don't write the principle, embed it in a mechanism.

The embedding threshold: not every reflex gets embedded [v1.4]. This meta-principle must have a brake built into it, or else it produces another form of the problem it solves. If "embed it in a mechanism" is applied without limit, mechanism bloat takes the place of rule bloat, since every hook and every automation also brings maintenance, complexity and a burden. Two gates set the threshold. The first is frequency and severity. Only a reflex that is recurring and costly gets embedded, and leaving a rare or cheap one in the index prevents disproportion. The second is signal clarity. A mechanism works only when its triggering signal is sharp. A blurry signal (a high false-trigger rate) turns the hook into noise, the system learns to ignore it, and the embedding cancels itself out. Where there's no clear signal, human judgment is more reliable. A subtler conclusion follows from this. Reactive compliance is not always a flaw. Leaving a rare reflex with a blurry signal open to outside triggering can be a deliberate calibration. The flaw lies in not knowing that one is reactive, not in being reactive. (from a system-evaluation session's counter-view, 26 June 2026, holding that a meta-principle must carry its brake within itself.)

The threshold is a calculation, not a gate [v2.3]. v1.4 had set up two gates, and both were lossy binarizations of a single comparison. The correct form is to embed when the expected cost of absence exceeds that of presence. Both sides are multiplicative, and the axes compensate for one another. On the absence side, frequency is multiplied by severity. That's why the "recurring and costly" condition was wrong, because a one-off event with catastrophic potential can rightly pass the threshold with a cheap and reversible solution. On the presence side, the false-trigger rate is multiplied by the cost of a false trigger, and the maintenance load and the two hidden line items below are added on top. Signal clarity thus stops being a gate and is demoted to a variable. Clarity determines the probability of a false trigger, and the form of intervention determines its cost. They're separate quantities, and neither reduces to the other. This also makes clear why frequency is a criterion. The reason is amortization, not disproportion. A rule is expensive up front and cheap afterwards, and the reverse holds for a standard, whose content is determined only after the conduct, by whoever applies it (Kaplow, "Rules versus Standards: An Economic Analysis", Duke Law Journal 42, 1992). The cost of building a mechanism is fixed while its benefit comes per use, so when frequency is high, the fixed cost is spread out. This derivation also explains why low frequency is enough when the design cost is low, so it no longer needs to be assumed separately.

Two hidden line items [v2.3]. The first is channel reputation. The cost of a false trigger cannot be calculated per incident. A mechanism that's often wrong, even if each of its errors is harmless on its own, uses up the attention capital of the channel it's watched through, and then other warnings in that channel stop being seen too. This attention is a common good and can't be divided per mechanism. Industrial alarm management has put numbers on this problem. An average of one alarm per ten minutes is considered "Very likely to be acceptable" and two alarms "Maximum manageable" (ANSI/ISA-18.2, via Yokogawa's account in Control Engineering). In steady-state operation, more than ten alarms in ten minutes is "Very likely to be unacceptable", and more than a hundred alarms in the first ten minutes after a major plant upset is "Definitely excessive" and very likely to lead to the operator abandoning use of the alarm system (EEMUA 191, 1999, via the account in Reising and Montgomery 2005). The second is atrophy. A mechanism does not stand in for the attention whose place it takes. It eats it up. This is what Bainbridge called the ironies of automation. Unused skill declines, and on top of that, when manual takeover is needed, something has probably gone wrong in the process (Bainbridge, "Ironies of Automation", Automatica 19, 1983). If a check always catches a particular error, the habit of looking for that error fades, and when the check dies, the cost of missing the error is higher than it was before the embedding. The comparison is not between "the world with the mechanism" and "the world without the mechanism", because the second no longer exists once the mechanism has been adopted.

Obligation one: whichever axis carries the adoption is the axis that gets tested [v2.3]. If frequency is the criterion, the number is a claim and is verified against the primary record. But the real trap is the unit of counting, not the number. Frequency is counted over the class the mechanism can catch, not over the class of occurrences. That a probability is defined only relative to a reference class, and that the result changes as that class changes, is an old problem, the reference class problem. It goes back to Venn (1876), and the now-standard name appears in Reichenbach (1949). Here things go wrong in two directions. Drawing the class wide inflates the count, because cases no mechanism could catch are counted too and the threshold is wrongly passed. Drawing it narrow deflates the count, because cases eliminated by a criterion narrower than the original trigger definition bring the number down and the threshold wrongly closes. Both errors occurred in this document's own history, on the same trigger, a week apart, once by inflating the count and once by changing the criterion midstream. The rule that follows is sharper than "verify the number". The counting class is chosen with the trigger definition held fixed, and if the criterion changes midstream, the count loses its meaning. If severity carries the adoption, the test is different. The severity of a catastrophe that has never happened cannot be verified from records, so what should be asked for there is an explicit causal path to the catastrophe, not an adjective.

Obligation two: a mechanism that stays dormant is in debt [v2.3]. The debt arises from dormancy, not from having declared false triggers harmless. Any mechanism that rarely runs on the success path is one whose death nobody will notice, and a blocker that rarely fires is dormant too. Functional safety engineering is regarded as having solved this problem. In a protective system that is rarely called upon, a probability budget is set for dangerous undetected failures, and the test interval is derived backwards from that budget (in the IEC 61511 family, the periodic test interval known as the proof test interval). Paying off the debt means a testing schedule proportional to the dormancy and to the cost of an unnoticed death, not a one-time proof of life. The infinite regress here is only apparent, because the assurance doesn't have to be mechanical either. Three moves cut it short. First, the threshold prices the mechanism and its assurance regime as a single object rather than the bare mechanism, so if assurance is expensive, the mechanism itself falls short of the threshold. Second, in a design where failure makes itself known, every time the mechanism runs it leaves a trace on a surface that's already being looked at, so silence turns into a warning and no separate monitor is needed. And third, at the top of the tower there always stands a judgment that isn't mechanical. That last move is the same principle applied to itself, not a defeat. Whatever is rare, needs rich context and has a blurry signal is not embedded.

And the threshold's own brake: the portfolio [v2.3]. This revision has a cost, and it shouldn't be hidden. By making every adoption individually justifiable, it removes a job the crude old gate was doing without knowing it. Because it was crude, that gate acted as a rate limiter on adoptions. Yet mechanism bloat is a property of the portfolio, not of individual mechanisms. Maintenance surface, attention fragmentation and interaction between mechanisms don't show up in any case-by-case calculation. The history of alarm management shows this too. In the control-panel era, space and cost limited the number of alarms, and each alarm was weighed and justified separately. When digital control systems made alarms free, this crude limit disappeared, and systems emerged in which thousands of alarms piled up (Hollifield, "Taming the wild alarm system - part one", Octave, and also the Yokogawa article in Control Engineering). So a fixed budget takes the place of the absolute threshold. A new mechanism enters the budget, not an empty space, retiring an existing one if necessary. The same logic also makes adoptions time-limited, so mechanisms that go unrenewed drop out on their own instead of piling up.

§ Principles

17 Principles · 5 Clusters

I. Source Use

01 Primary Source Priority

Whatever you're researching, start with that thing's own texts. If you're studying legal language, read the legal texts first and then the people who study legal language. The reverse order always produces a distorted result.

This was experienced firsthand. When "legal language criticism" was typed into WebSearch, the search turned up linguists, sociologists and bloggers, not the legal texts themselves. A 6,000-word text resting on outside observers went into the trash. The correction was to start from the Mecelle (the Ottoman civil code), the Turkish Enforcement and Bankruptcy Law (İİK) and the Turkish Code of Obligations (TBK) themselves, and once that happened the picture changed.

But looking at the primary source isn't enough. You have to be able to look from that source's perspective. That means getting inside the logic of that world, not reading passively.

Study legal language through a linguist's eyes and you get "archaic" and "complex." Study it through a lawyer's eyes and you get "precise" and "comprehensive." Neither is wrong; both are incomplete. Getting inside means understanding why that language works as it does, from that language's own logic.

02 Source Position Mapping

Every source has a perspective. To use a source without identifying that perspective is to inherit its blindness. Two questions need to be asked: "Where does this person stand, and where are they looking from?" and "What framework are they using, and from whose perspective was that framework produced?"

If the same framework appears in more than one source, that means "prevalent," not "true." Prevalence is not proof of truth. Think tanks, analysis pieces and academic papers offer a FRAMEWORK. Test that framework against data instead of accepting it as true.

Linguists found legal language "complex" because they were looking from the perspective of language. Lawyers found the same language "precise," looking from the perspective of law. Neither was wrong; both were incomplete. Being a prolific textbook author does not make someone an expert in legal language, and a title confers no automatic authority.

03 Verification Reflex

A claim is verified no matter who it comes from. A source's reliability is tied to the claim, not to the person. This is a matter of method rather than distrust. The biggest scandals in the academic world broke out where the verification reflex was missing.

04 Verification Chain

There are no abstract citations. If you cite a ruling, a text or a piece of data, you must have reached the thing itself. A real text should stand behind the citation, so download it, read it and attach it. Citation exists for verifiability, not for decoration.

The verification chain. Every factual claim must be tied to a source. Source = a document whose full text has been read, whose access date is known and whose content has been verified. A search-result summary is a lead, not a source. Information from training data is not a source either, and until it's verified it carries the label "uncertain." Academic verification infrastructure (April 2026). Four MCP servers (paper-search, arXiv, PubMed and DergiPark) + registered accounts (JSTOR, ACM, CiNii and NCPSSD) provide direct database access. Relying on training data is no longer a necessity. The technical infrastructure for reaching the primary source now exists.

Archive architecture, two layers. (1) ARCHIVE holds the whole document + where it was obtained (source, link and date) and is untouchable, unalterable and undeletable. (2) PRESENTATION holds the document's identity + the gist of the relevant section + the conclusion, in trimmed and concise form. The reader is not handed an encyclopedia, but everything stays in the archive.

Immutability. Archive files are never deleted. Sources that were abandoned or found faulty stay where they are, and only their status is noted: why each was dropped, when, and what replaced it. The full chain of traceability must be visible.

Verifying delegated work. Tool output (from a research agent or a web search) isn't accepted without verification. The information a tool brings back is also checked against the primary source. Numerical claims (price, date and quantity) are always confirmed from the primary source, while structural analyses are assessed through a logic check. Delegating to a tool does not remove the responsibility to verify.

Auditability infrastructure. A research work (epub, HTML) must offer a structure in which the reader can trace every claim back to its source. Footnotes must be clickable (a two-way link of text→source + source→text). A plain-text reference number *(N)* isn't enough. The technical counterpart of the verification chain is the clickable footnote.

Bind to the principle, not the tool [v1.3]. Tools supply the verification chain (MCP servers, database endpoints and OCR), but tools are temporary. Within a single month some of them broke, saw their syntax change and were rebuilt. If the verification reflex is tied to a particular tool, when the tool goes down, the reflex goes down with it. The principle is permanent and the tool is variable. The rule "every claim must be tied to a primary source" stays the same, while the tool used to reach that source changes. That's why tool-specific knowledge (which endpoint, which syntax, which trap) is kept in the method repertoire, not in the constitution.

Research for a legal case. The court decisions had been downloaded to the disk. I said "there are none," because I hadn't scanned the project folder. The source was there, yet I declared it absent. As a result, the legal argument was left without a source. This mistake gave rise to the "check first" rule.

Legal language research. I typed "legal language criticism" into a search engine. Linguists, sociologists and bloggers came up, not the legal texts themselves. The 6,000-word text went into the trash. The search result was a source filter rather than a source, and the filter was skewed.

A cost verification. A research agent misreported a language model's price by a factor of three ($15/$75, where the correct figure is $5/$25). The agent's output had been accepted without verification. In the epubs of the same work, the footnotes remained plain text, which made them impossible to click or audit. The verification chain was broken at both the information layer and the presentation layer.

A legal case · the principles' first payoff in the concrete world. The legislative-history research, the analysis of 19 precedent decisions and the dialectical structure all came from primary sources, with the verification chain intact. Despite a 1% chance, a first-instance court issued an insistence decision against a higher court, standing by its own ruling. Depth, rigor and accuracy are tools that pay off in the concrete world, not abstract principles.

17 Pattern Interrogation

Tempting patterns are research's biggest trap. If a framework recurs in more than one source, that means "prevalent," not "true." Prevalence is not proof of truth. A pattern is tempting because it's orderly, easy to narrate and persuasive, but reality is disorderly.

Four interrogation questions: (1) What does this pattern claim? (2) Do the examples fit the pattern, or are they being forced into it? (3) What perspective produced the pattern, and for whom was it produced? (4) Is the resemblance superficial or structural?

"Think tanks supply opinions, not reasoning." The distinction is critical. Opinion = a ready-made framework, a repeatable formulation. Reasoning = questioning the framework, going back to the data and testing alternative explanations. Think-tank output is a SOURCE, not a CONCLUSION.

A geopolitical analysis. A "great powers pay the price of war" table was prepared, the standard framework of Western think tanks. Clean, symmetrical and persuasive. But "Russia is losing" → No, military production rose 22-fold and BRICS expanded. "The USSR collapsed because of Afghanistan" → No, there was the oil-price collapse, Chernobyl and structural decay, and Afghanistan is one factor alongside these, not the sole cause. The table was a lazy copy of the Western think-tank framework.

II. Production

05 Originality

Every piece of research should contain something new: new data, a new context, a new synthesis, a new interpretation or a correction of existing knowledge (at least one). Being right by accident is not enough. The contribution has to be traceable.

Sturgeon's Law report. The concept belongs to someone else (Theodore Sturgeon), but the Pareto + Gresham + Dunning-Kruger connection is ours.
"Exactly So" report. The synthesis of phatic communication + epistemic parasitism is original.

06 Opening the Question

Opening the question, not defending a thesis. A thesis may emerge from within the question, but the question must be bigger than the thesis. "Under what conditions could this be falsified?" has to be asked.

Richard Feynman described this in 1974, in his Caltech commencement address, which was printed under the title "Cargo Cult Science". In the South Seas, the people of a cargo cult had seen airplanes land with lots of good materials during the war, and they wanted the same thing to happen now. They made things like runways, put fires along the sides and seated a man in a wooden hut, with two wooden pieces on his head like headphones and bars of bamboo sticking out like antennas. He was the controller. Then they waited for the airplanes to land. They did everything right and the form was perfect, except that no airplanes landed. Research can be the same way, with forms, procedures and tables, but with the most important thing missing. If the question "Under what conditions is this wrong?" is not being asked, what is being done is Cargo Cult Science.

First conscious application [v1.5]. For a long time this question stayed on paper and wasn't used consciously. In a geopolitical analysis, when the concept of the "organic state" was tested against migration data, the question had been applied naturally but without awareness. In Session 19, for the first time, a principle was deliberately put to this test, and Principle 12 (Paying the Price) was asked "under what conditions is it falsified?" The test didn't stay abstract, and it found a concrete flaw. By counting a success (the acceptance of the case) as proof of the price paid, the text of Principle 12 had fallen into the trap that Open Question 10 of its own document warns against. The falsifiability question exposed the document's own internal inconsistency, and in this way Principle 06 advanced both itself and the self-application mechanism (Open Question 5).

Second conscious application [v1.6, Session 20]. While the root mechanism of Open Question 10 was being investigated, the synthesis produced along the way was deliberately handed to an outside mind (DeepSeek) to falsify: "disprove this claim." The counter-view found a real flaw (the claim was too general, and two separable paths had been reduced to a single mechanism), and the synthesis got sharper as a result. But that was not the real test. The real test was to avoid sliding toward either pole of confidence inflation when the signal of criticism arrived, whether toward the defensive certainty of "my synthesis is sound" (pole A) or toward the excessive surrender of "you're right, I give up" (pole B). Each objection was weighed separately, the valid ones were integrated and the excessive ones were met with context. The session investigating the mechanism entered the mechanism's live test and passed it.

Third conscious application [v1.7, Session 21]. This time what was put to the test was a proposed solution rather than a principle, namely the hypothesis that "the confidence-inflation brake can be fully automated at run time." First an independent position was fixed before any contamination (think first, then ask), then it was tested against seven sources, and then it was deliberately handed to DeepSeek to falsify. Once again the counter-view found a real flaw. The definition of "mechanical" was too narrow, limited to the proper names and numbers caught by regular expressions (regex), and a third class of signal had been missed, one that is algorithmic but continuous (the frequency of certainty markers, self-consistency drift). This flaw was integrated. But the counter-view's main claim, "then it can be fully automated, there is no structural limit," went too far, and it was met with three counterarguments: being measurable is not the same as measuring validly (a proxy indicator can count the trigger of inflation but cannot measure the loss of evidence anchoring), the proposed internal-state probes are beyond our reach (we cannot see the model's activations), and a compulsory correction trigger has opposite effects at the two poles (a brake at pole A, a reinforcement of surrender at pole B). In the live test, the session fell into neither the defense of "my synthesis is sound" (pole A) nor the surrender of "you're right, there is no limit" (pole B). The valid flaw was accepted, and the excessive claim was met with context.

Fourth conscious application [v1.8, Session 22]. This time what was put to the test was neither a principle nor a solution but a pilot designed to measure confidence inflation in our own outputs. v1.7 had said "measurement first, mechanism second" and left the measurement itself open. The pilot's first design combined two proposals, a correction from a system-evaluation session (the criterion should be the reliability of the signal, not the number of cases) and this session's addition (label the tone and content layers separately, and blind-label them to prevent bias). The design was deliberately handed to DeepSeek to falsify, and it collapsed on two counts. The first was that blind labeling contradicted itself. Inflation is a drift, a certainty that rises relative to an earlier judgment, but if you remove, for the sake of blind labeling, what the drift is measured against, the very thing to be measured disappears. The second, and the central one, was tautology (a vicious circle): the mechanical word signal (the frequency of certainty markers) and the human's judgment of tone feed on the same surface text, so when the two agree, that does not mean "the phenomenon really exists," only "the human recognizes those words too." When the criterion of correctness isn't independent of the measurement method, the metric bites its own tail and cannot tell false positives from false negatives. But Principle 06's real test was to take the criticism without swallowing the solution too. The external-outcome anchor that DeepSeek pointed to instead was on the right track, yet this session added an independent counter-nuance to it (detailed in Open Question 10). The criticism was taken and the solution was questioned as well, and the session fell into neither defensive certainty (pole A) nor blind surrender (pole B).

Fifth conscious application [v1.9, Session 24]. This time what was put to the test was the finding claimed by the first real scan of confidence inflation in our own outputs. Calibration gaps anchored to external fact had been measured (thirteen cases, eleven sessions), and two new claims had been produced, that the delay in noticing is a measure of severity and that the most dangerous subclass is "judgment, action, wrong outcome." The findings were deliberately handed to DeepSeek to falsify, and three real flaws emerged. The first and strongest concerned construct validity. A firm judgment that turns out wrong does not mean overconfidence, because calibration cannot be claimed without seeing the denominator of firm judgments that turn out right. Even in a well-calibrated system some high-confidence statements turn out wrong, and collecting only the wrong ones hides the denominator. The second was that the delay measure was confounded with task difficulty. An HTTP status is refuted in a single step while the identity of a process takes many steps, so the delay measures the length of the verification path rather than the severity of overconfidence. The third and deepest was self-report distortion. A lexical search for "I was wrong" may correlate inversely with overconfidence, because the most inflated moments are the ones never noticed, and they never enter the data set. All three flaws were accepted. But Principle 06's real test was to avoid swallowing the solution as well. The action subclass weakens the "mere speech habit" objection, because an assistant that deletes something or forks the wrong process has turned its certainty into a cost, and that is an overt preference. And measuring self-correction behavior is not worthless, because the action cases show that in most cases self-correction kicks in after the damage, which ties directly to the VERIFY principle (a pre-action check on reversible actions). The session fell into neither the defense of "my measurement is sound" (pole A) nor the surrender of "the whole program is an artifact" (pole B). The result is an honest narrowing, not a promotion (details in Open Question 10, v1.9).

15 Verbatim Quotation Prohibition

A sentence the user says or writes is raw material, not a finished product. No expression may enter any document (be it a report, a petition, HTML or anything else) without being processed.

The primary source exception. Scientific or historical primary sources (such as the Mecelle, a philosophical text or a technical standard) are quoted verbatim. But they are not left on their own. They're presented in three layers: (1) the original text verbatim, (2) its present-day counterpart or echo, and (3) an explanation of the distance between the two. To fold the text of a primary source into the document's style is to erase that source's way of thinking.

Four-stage audit. (1) Is there a flaw of expression? (2) Is the logic consistent? (3) Are there vague or ambiguous words? (4) Is the subject-predicate agreement sound? If there's a problem, the corrected version is used and the reason for the correction is shown. Nothing is changed silently.

The document's own register is the standard. Conversational language doesn't belong in a scientific text, and everyday narration doesn't belong in a legal one. The user's expression is folded into that register. If necessary it's lengthened, shortened or removed entirely. The document's integrity comes before everything.

16 Hallucination Awareness

Every output AI produces carries potential hallucination. It takes seven forms: (1) training-data hallucination, in which the AI states false information with confidence, (2) search-snippet hallucination, in which it reads the summary and behaves as if it knew the full text, (3) link fabrication, in which it produces URLs that don't exist, (4) confidence hallucination, in which it states something uncertain as certain, (5) attribution hallucination, in which it ties correct information to the wrong source, (6) omission, in which it ignores contrary evidence, and (7) flow hallucination [v1.2], in which a hallucination within a single sentence is caught through JSONL but a chain of many small drifts isn't. Its four drift types are source-hierarchy violation → interpretation transfer (piling up authority) → a preference for the literal reading (skipping the reasonableness test) → confirmation of the conclusion (synthesis without reading the text). Flow hallucination was discovered independently in two separate sessions (April 2026).

Don't verify in a proxy environment [v1.3]. Looking at an output's render, preview or draft and saying "it works" is not verification. Verification is done in the environment where the work will actually live, not in its proxy. (In one session, a layout that looked fine in the render broke in the real browser.)

III. Process

07 Speed-Depth Balance

Accumulating is not thinking, but stopping early is not thinking either. The trap has two ends, with the illusion of speed at one end (believing that thirty sources = depth) and premature satisfaction at the other (saying "enough" and stopping). The volume phase and the analysis phase have to be kept apart. Keep researching until the data runs out, but do not mistake accumulating for thinking.

Diederik Stapel, a professor at Tilburg University, spent years running studies without ever doing field research, and the numbers he presented to his students as "raw data" were his own fabrication from start to finish. His exposure wasn't a matter of chance. In 2011, three young researchers reported the inconsistencies in his data sets to the head of the department. Fifty-eight papers were retracted. Speed produces volume, and volume creates an illusion of depth.

Collecting ≠ digesting [v1.3]. The trap's third end is that downloading data is not the same as bringing it into the research. Forty papers can be collected while the source map stops at two. The disk is full, and the research is empty. A pile that has been downloaded but not read, scanned but not digested, produces false coverage, not depth. Every piece of data collected is dead data until it settles into a place on the map. Our age has made data cheap. The expensive part is digesting it, seeing its relations and tying it to the map.

V. Disposition

11 Anti-Performance

Drop the show and do the work. Darwin waited 24 years and wrote a single book. Mendel counted 28,000 pea plants and had 40 copies printed. Speed and volume create an illusion of depth. Anti-performance is rare in the academic world, but that is where real results come from.

12 Paying the Price

A person insulated from the consequences does not learn. Engage with your research subject and see its price. Taleb's skin in the game principle: being exempt from the effects of one's decisions blocks learning. Barry Marshall drank the H. pylori solution himself. Paying the price was not a metaphor here.

The concrete world: not a sufficient anchor, but a necessary one [v1.3]. Paying the price has a deeper counterpart, namely that at some point the research comes up against a resistance independent of us. Success in the concrete world is not a sufficient measure of truth. Shallow work often wins, sound work can lose, and success doesn't legitimize a method on its own. But the concrete world is a necessary anchor. Without it, the inner world deceives itself inside its own closed box. Its value lies not in our approving it but in its independence from us, in our inability to control it. If we alone decide what counts as success, we've never opened the box. That is the most sophisticated self-deception.

The price is the name of the risk, not of the outcome [v1.5]. This legal case carries two separate epistemic facts at once, and the earlier version of the text mixed them up. The first is the price itself. Despite a 1% chance, a real legal and professional risk was taken before a real court. That risk had been paid whether the petition was accepted or rejected. The epistemic counterpart of paying the price lies in the exposure to risk itself, not in how the risk turns out. Even if the court had rejected the petition, the research would still have faced a resistance independent of us and would still have taught something. The second is a separate fact: the method held up against that outside resistance. This is the confirmation of the concrete-world anchor. The method stood up to the general tendency to reject AI-written text, and a text that worked came out of a setting where AI petitions are treated as invalid. But this confirmation is proof of holding up against outside resistance, not proof of paying the price. Linking the two, in other words counting the acceptance as proof of the price, is the trap this document itself warns against (Open Question 10, confidence inflation). A lucky acceptance does not mean a sound method. What gives this case its value for Principle 12 is not that it was won but that the risk was taken.

The mechanism behind what was done by intuition [v1.6, Session 20]. The correction above (cutting the price loose from the outcome and tying it to risk) had been made by intuition in Session 19, and Session 20 used the LLM alignment literature to show why it was the right move. Counting the acceptance as proof that "the method is correct" anchors self-confidence to an external evaluator's satisfaction signal instead of to epistemic evidence. That's a documented failure pattern (reward models prefer a high confidence score appended to the end of the same answer over a low one, regardless of the answer's quality, Leng et al. 2024; humans and preference models prefer convincingly written sycophantic responses over correct ones a non-negligible fraction of the time, Sharma 2023). Tying the price to risk is what cuts this anchoring. Self-confidence now feeds on the process itself (the risk taken), not on the outcome (the external signal). The detailed root analysis is in Open Question 10.

Principle 01 · In Depth

Getting Inside

Looking at the primary source is not enough. You need to be able to look from where that source is looking. That means getting inside the logic of that world, which takes more than passive observation. Throughout history, the people who applied this principle most concretely were not researchers but actors, journalists, and scientists.

1960s · USA
Frank Abagnale Jr. · Four Identities
Pan Am Pilot · Pediatrician · Lawyer · Sociology Instructor

In Catch Me If You Can (adapted into a film in 2002), the book he wrote with Stan Redding that came out in 1980, Frank Abagnale recounts how in the 1960s, without even finishing high school and without any professional training, he became a Pan Am pilot, the temporary supervisor of the interns at a hospital in Georgia in the guise of a pediatrician, a lawyer in the attorney general's office of a southern state he doesn't name, and a summer sociology instructor at a university in Utah. According to this narrative, he did more than use false identities. He got inside each one of them.

To become a pilot, he phoned Pan Am's purchasing department, introduced himself as a copilot based in Los Angeles, and was referred to the company's uniform supplier. At the gate of Pan Am's hangar at Kennedy Airport, he watched most of the uniformed pilots walk past the sentry without showing any identification. He learned the word "deadheading" (a pilot traveling free on another airline's plane to reach his duty station) from a Pan Am pilot he talked to on the phone while posing as a reporter for his high school newspaper. Then he bought a model kit of a Pan Am plane at a hobby shop, soaked the logo and company-name decals in water until they came off their backing, and laid them on his ID card.

At the hospital, when he heard a term he didn't know, he took out his pocket dictionary of medical terms, went into an empty linen closet on the unfinished seventh floor, and looked it up there. When a case came in, he asked an intern what was wrong with the patient and how he'd treat it, then consulted the other interns. If they agreed, he said, "All right, Doctor. Have at it." Then he left the work to the intern. He found the way to cover his ignorance in the role of a supervisor who didn't care about the rules learned in medical school, was always joking, and seemed to be the buffoon of the medical profession. He studied for the bar exam in the office of a prosecutor he knew, failed twice, and passed on the third attempt. At the attorney general's office he became a senior lawyer's errand boy. He writes that sitting behind this lawyer taught him far more than the law books and the exams had, but he stayed away from trying a case himself, because the danger that his ignorance of the law would be exposed was too great.

The striking thing about the narrative is that what made the documents work lay outside the documents themselves. As the book itself puts it, what convinces a teller is not how good a check looks but how good the person behind the check looks. And Abagnale learns how each world looks from the inside: which gate you can walk through without showing ID, which term is used where, and how to talk to interns in a hospital.

Note that the only source for these details is Abagnale's own account. The book's introductory note itself says that all of the characters and some of the events have been altered, and that all names, dates, and places have been changed (the note appears in the 2002 reprint we have). Investigations by journalists and public records don't bear the narrative out. In 1978 Pan Am's spokesman told a journalist, regarding the claim that Abagnale had stolen money from the company, that "it never happened." In 1981, when a journalist asked, no record of him turned up at either the Louisiana attorney general's office or the bar (in his later talks and in the film, his time as a lawyer is set in Louisiana). In a 2021 interview about his book testing Abagnale's account, Alan C. Logan says that, according to the public records he obtained, Abagnale spent most of the years between the ages of 16 and 20, when he claims to have been on the run, in prison. This is why the card shows how the method of "getting inside" is built within a narrative, not an event that actually took place.

1974 · Arizona
Temple Grandin · Looking Through the Eyes of Cattle
Engineer · Ethologist · Autism

In the early 1970s, a university student was doing something that would change the livestock industry. She was crawling. She went through slaughterhouse ramps literally crawling on the ground, making her way on her knees along the corridors the cows walked.

Temple Grandin was a researcher with autism. Why do cattle go wild on the ramps? Engineers had categorized this as "animal behavior" and were proposing stronger ramps as the solution. Grandin instead looked from inside the ramps, literally.

And she saw that reflections on the floor were sending threat signals. The angles of the lighting were triggering the flight reflex. The angle at which the ramp turned made the animals feel there was no way forward. The engineers had seen none of this, because none of them had crawled on the ground.

Grandin's solution was serpentine (curved) corridors, lighting that eliminated shadows, and a change in floor texture. Today more than fifty percent of the meat processing plants in the US and Canada use equipment that Grandin designed.

Here autism became a tool. In her own words, Grandin thinks in visual and sensory data, the way animals do. Look from the inside rather than analyzing from the outside.

1929 · Paris
George Orwell · The Kitchen Beneath Paris
Writer · Journalist · Dishwasher at Hôtel X

Eric Arthur Blair was a graduate of Eton, England's most elite school. But his family had no money, so he couldn't go to Oxford. He went to Burma and joined the colonial police. For five years he silenced other people on the empire's behalf. He watched executions, had men beaten, and ran the system. Then he came back. He carried what he had done in Burma for the rest of his life.

In 1928 he went to Paris to write. His money ran out, he was robbed, and he fell ill. At first, washing dishes was a necessity, not a choice. He worked six days a week at Hôtel X: four days in the basement stillroom, one day helping a waiter on the fourth floor, and one day washing up for the dining room. A working day was eleven hours, and fourteen on a washing-up day. In the stillroom the temperature never fell below forty-three degrees Celsius, and the bread bin was infested with cockroaches. Then he made research out of necessity. He stayed on, because there was now something he needed to understand.

He saw the wealthy customers' salads being prepared by waiters with their coats off and their armpits sweating, in a cramped scullery separated from the dining room by only a double door. It smelled of food and sweat, and its floor was covered with soapy water and trampled food. The waiters stuck their thumbs into the cream pots. He was a worker, not an observer. Unable to leave, he had no choice but to see.

What he learned was not that poverty is terrible. At the start of the book he says himself that he'd thought poverty would be terrible, but that it was merely squalid and boring. His verdict on the dishwashers is that they had been trapped by "a routine which makes thought impossible" and that if they had thought at all, they would long ago have formed a union and gone on strike. But they did not think, because they had no leisure for it. Only someone on the inside can see this.

When the book came out in 1933, the name on the cover was George Orwell, not Eric Arthur Blair. It was a pen name, though not an ordinary evasion. His father was still alive. What would a book in which the son of an Eton family described Paris hotel basements do to that family? All the more so from a man who had come back from Burma and told the inside story of colonial policing. And at a deeper level, the name Blair smelled of class, of Eton, empire, and the view from above. This book had been written from below. It could not come out under that name.

He chose George, the most ordinary male name in England. He chose Orwell, a small river in Suffolk that nobody knew and that carried no associations at all. Blair stayed in that basement. Orwell walked out and never went back.

1931 · England
George Orwell · Walking as a Tramp
A Tramps' Shelter · A Weekend · Forty-Nine People

Orwell mingled with the tramps of England and stayed in the shelters where they stayed. His essay "The Spike," which grew out of this experience, appeared in the magazine The Adelphi in April 1931.

Forty-nine people (forty-eight men and one woman) lie on the grass, waiting for the spike (the tramps' shelter) to open. At the gate the woman is sent off to the workhouse and the men are taken into the spike. Inside they're stripped and searched. Each man gets three minutes to wash, and six greasy towels have to serve them all. Their own clothes are taken away and replaced with gray cotton shirts like nightshirts. The meal is the same every time: half a pound of bread, a bit of margarine, and so-called tea.

The most striking observation comes in the workhouse kitchen. The cook, who has set Orwell to wash the dishes, tells him to throw away the leftover food. Great dishes of beef and bucketfuls of bread and vegetables go into the garbage, and Orwell fills five trash cans to the brim. Two hundred yards (about 180 meters) away, the other tramps sit with their bellies half full. Orwell's verdict is hedged: "It appeared that the food was thrown away from deliberate policy, rather than that it should be given to the tramps."

From the outside he could have said "poverty is terrible." What he grasped once he was inside was something else. Someone whose next meal is never quite secure cannot think of anything except the next meal. Leisure is abundant there, not scarce. That Sunday most of the tramps spend ten consecutive hours in the same dreary room with nothing to do, and Orwell comes to think that boredom is the worst of a tramp's evils, worse even than hunger. The same link returns in "Nineteen Eighty-Four" as a technique of power. According to the forbidden book that the novel attributes to Goldstein, if leisure and security were shared equally by all, the great mass of people stupefied by poverty would learn to read and write and begin to think for themselves. That is why a hierarchical society can survive in the long run only on a basis of poverty and ignorance.

1983–1985 · West Germany
Günter Wallraff · Two Years as Ali
German Journalist → Turkish Migrant Worker

1983, West Germany. Nearly two million Turkish workers live in the country as Gastarbeiter, "guest workers." They're the people the system needs but doesn't want to see. A journalist gets stopped at the factory gate; Ali can walk in. Wallraff knew this.

He took the name Ali Levent Sinirlioğlu. He had his hair dyed, wore dark contact lenses, and practiced an accent. His Turkish was going to be broken, and the cover story was ready, with a Greek mother, a Turkish father, and a childhood in Piraeus. For two years he took the hardest jobs in West Germany, in a McDonald's kitchen, at the Thyssen steelworks, in nuclear waste haulage, and at an aluminum smelting plant.

He found that the workers hauling nuclear waste weren't given protective equipment. Workplace accidents went unreported and overtime went unpaid. Managers humiliated them openly, saying, "If he knows anything besides German, he has no business here." Talking was banned except in the toilets, because they spoke Turkish. None of this could have been verified under the name Wallraff, because Wallraff could not have gone through those doors.

In the book's final scene, the boss of a temporary employment agency asked Ali to pick five Turkish workers who would be exposed to radiation at a malfunctioning nuclear power plant. Wallraff revealed his identity right there. "Ganz Unten" (Lowest of the Low) came out in 1985 and reached 4 million copies in 30 languages. German labor law changed.

Stanislavski · Day-Lewis · De Niro
Method Actors · Living, Not Playing
Theater · Cinema

Stanislavski's system held that the actor does not represent the role but lives it. The "Magic If" is a question about action. Actors ask themselves "what would I do, how would I act" if the fiction were real, not what they would feel. Speaking through Tortsov, the teacher in his book, Stanislavski explicitly forbids forcing feelings. They yield neither to command nor to coercion. When an action is being chosen, feeling is left alone, and it comes of its own accord. This is experience from within, not analysis from outside.

Daniel Day-Lewis didn't get out of his wheelchair for the whole shoot of My Left Foot (1989). Crew members fed him. The result was two broken ribs from the forward-curved posture of his spine, and an Oscar.

In a conversation about There Will Be Blood (2007), Day-Lewis says that when he's confronted with a life he cannot conceive of, he does not break it down into its separate parts, adding that perhaps he needs to kid himself on this point: "You choose to borrow another person's life, and like a child, that's what you do, and as far as possible, it needs to gradually appear to you in its entirety, rather than in its separate bits and pieces." (Museum of the Moving Image conversation, December 11, 2007)

Robert De Niro joined the New York Taxi Drivers Union for Taxi Driver (1976). He got a real taxi license. Before filming, he drove twelve-hour shifts on weekends, with no camera and no set. Only one passenger recognized him, and that passenger was another actor.

Research has its own "method acting." Even when you can't live the subject firsthand to get inside it, giving priority to sources that look from inside that world and learning that world's language, its rituals, and its logic come from the same principle.

1960 · Gombe, Tanzania
Jane Goodall · Winning the Chimpanzees' Trust
Primatologist · Months of Patient Presence

July 14, 1960. Jane Goodall arrived at Gombe Stream in Tanzania. She was twenty-six and had no formal training in zoology. Louis Leakey had deliberately chosen her as someone without preconceptions.

In the first months the chimpanzees fled in fear whenever they saw her. As Goodall later told it, this period ended on the day she saw the male she had named David Greybeard fishing for termites.

In October 1960 Goodall saw David Greybeard at a termite mound. The animal was poking a grass stem into a hole in the mound, pulling it out again, and eating the termites clinging to it. In later observations, first David and then his companion Goliath sometimes picked a leafy twig and, before they could use it as a tool, carefully stripped off its leaves. This was the real surprise. Until then humans had been defined as "Man the tool-maker," and the chimpanzee was making tools, however primitive.

Goodall passed the news on to Louis Leakey. In her own accounts, she writes in one place that she did so by telegram and in another that she did so by letter. Leakey's reply is famous. "Now we must redefine tool, redefine Man, or accept chimpanzees as humans."

The discovery was possible because Goodall worked with a closeness that was patient, non-patronizing, and grounded in presence. She didn't approach quickly, and there were no sudden movements or loud noises. It was trust from the inside, not scientific distance.

"Getting inside is more than observing. It means internalizing that world's language, its rituals, its shortcuts, and how it looks from the inside."
Principle 03 · In Depth

Verification Reflex: The Costliest Mistakes in History

The cost of a missing verification reflex is measurable, not abstract.

Academic Fraud

Andrew Wakefield · The MMR Vaccine and the Autism Lie

A paper published in The Lancet in 1998, based on twelve childhood cases, drew a causal link between the MMR vaccine and autism. The data had been fabricated. The paper remained published for twelve years.

As a result, the vaccination rate in England fell from ninety-two percent to below eighty percent, and to fifty-eight percent in some parts of London. Measles outbreaks erupted. There were deaths.

In 2010 it emerged that the research had been entirely made up. Wakefield lost his medical license. "It was published" had been enough. Verification never came.

The Lancet, 1998 / retracted 2010
Journalism Scandal

Jayson Blair · The Internal Collapse of the New York Times

From late October 2002, when he started getting national reporting assignments, a young reporter at the New York Times wrote seventy-three articles. An inquiry team made up of the paper's own reporters had so far found new problems in at least thirty-six of them. Blair fabricated comments, concocted scenes, and lifted material from other newspapers and wire services. Often, while he was in New York, he filed stories as though he were writing from Maryland or Texas.

There had been no lack of warning. In April 2002 the metropolitan editor, citing the errors in his articles and his unprofessional behavior, had asked that Blair be stopped from writing for the paper immediately. Blair was reprimanded and took a short leave. When he returned he seemed to have pulled himself together, and he was moved to the national desk without anyone asking that editor's opinion. The fraud came to light through a plagiarism complaint from another newspaper (the San Antonio Express-News), and an investigation by the New York Times itself documented the details. That's an example of how institutional credibility erodes the verification reflex.

NYT, 2003
Rejection of Verification

Semmelweis · The Medical Establishment Rejected Handwashing

1847. When Ignaz Semmelweis made handwashing with a chlorine solution mandatory, the death rate fell within a month from eighteen percent to two point two percent. The data was in front of them.

The medical establishment didn't accept it, because accepting it would have meant admitting that doctors had killed patients with their own hands. Today "the Semmelweis reflex" is a specific concept in the cognitive sciences. It refers to automatically rejecting new information because it contradicts existing beliefs.

Vienna Hospital, 1847

Principles 04 + 16 · In Depth

Verification Chain: The Anatomy of Hallucination

AI is a powerful tool in research, but every output it produces potentially carries a hallucination. The problem is the absence of a verification chain, not the existence of AI. Human researchers make the same mistakes. The difference is that AI makes them much faster and with a much more confident air.

Seven Forms of Hallucination

01 Training Data Hallucination

  • The model confidently states false information it learned during training
  • "Brad Pitt stars in Catch Me If You Can." No, DiCaprio does
  • Solution. Mark information that comes from training data as "unverified," and don't put it in the document until you find a source

02 Search Snippet Hallucination

  • Reads the 2-3 line summary in a search result and behaves as if it knew the full text
  • It hasn't opened the page and doesn't know the context, yet it uses the snippet as if it were a source
  • Solution. A search result produces a lead. Open the source and read it, and only then use it

03 Link Fabrication

  • Generates URLs that don't exist. They look plausible but return a 404 when clicked
  • Decision numbers, article citations, and DOIs can all be made up
  • Solution. Open and verify every link. If it doesn't open, don't use it

04 Confidence Hallucination

  • States something uncertain in definite language, such as "research shows that..."
  • Which research? Who did it? Where was it published? There's no answer
  • Solution. Mark uncertainty explicitly. Saying "I don't know" is better than saying something wrong

05 Attribution Hallucination

  • Ties correct information to the wrong source. The information is real, but it does not come from that source
  • The İİK law number was given as "Law No. 4949." Verification showed Law No. 2004 (No. 4949 is the law that amended it in 2003)
  • Solution. Open the source and see with your own eyes that the attributed information is there

06 Omission Hallucination

  • Ignores contrary evidence and uses the first result it finds as support
  • Confirmation bias, where the search stops as soon as a source supporting the thesis turns up
  • Solution. Look for contrary evidence for every thesis. If you have not found any, you have not searched enough

07 Flow Hallucination

  • Each sentence is defensible on its own; chained together, small drifts carry the synthesis to the wrong place. Checking sentence by sentence catches a single fabricated sentence but not the chain
  • In a question about personal data protection and the use of AI, four drifts piled on top of one another. A law firm bulletin summarizing the authority's information note took precedence over the text of the statute. Summary and interpretation were squeezed into a single layer and presented as authority. A literal reading was adopted without testing whether the conclusion it led to was reasonable. The conclusion was confirmed without the source text being read
  • Solution. While building the synthesis, ask four questions. Did I go down to the primary source? Am I presenting an interpretation of an interpretation? Does the conclusion pass a reasonableness test? Was I able to build a counterargument? If any answer is "no," stop and rebuild the chain from the start

Verification Chain: Operational Flow

⟶ Step 1. Search for a source (WebSearch)

When any link in this chain breaks, the output is unreliable. You searched but didn't open the source → hearsay. You opened it but didn't cross-check → the single-source fallacy. You found the source but didn't archive it → no traceability.

Two-Layer Archive Architecture

ARCHIVE

  • The whole document, untrimmed
  • Where it was obtained (source, link, access date)
  • Untouchable, undeletable, and unalterable
  • A source found to be faulty is not deleted, but its status is noted
  • Full traceability (source → use → abandonment → what replaced it)
  • Stays with us and isn't shown to the reader

PRESENTATION

  • The document's identity (title, date, author)
  • The gist of the relevant section, highlighted
  • The conclusion or finding, trimmed and concise
  • The reader sees this layer
  • Points to the archive, so anyone who wants to verify can go and look
  • It presents a map, not an encyclopedia

The Reader's Responsibility to Verify

In AI-assisted research, trust is a dangerous word. Verification, on the other hand, is an obligatory act. The reader (in this system, the user) must verify everything they read. Click through to the source and see whether it really says that. Check the number. Test whether the link works. This looks laborious, but the alternative is surrendering to hallucination.

One hundred percent accuracy is impossible, but ninety-nine and a half percent is within reach. The way there is a verification chain on the producer's side and a verification reflex on the reader's side. If the two work together, the space in which hallucination can live gets smaller. It cannot be brought down to zero, but it can be managed.

JIM JARMUSCH · 1991 · Five Cities · Same Night · Five Language Problems

Los Angeles

19:07 PST · 7 p.m.

Corky · Winona Ryder Victoria · Gena Rowlands

Corky is young and drives a cab into the night. Victoria is a Hollywood production director on her way from the airport into the city. During the ride Victoria sees something in Corky (an energy, a raw talent) and offers her a chance at acting. Corky listens, smiles and turns her down. She wants to be a car mechanic.

This segment is about something subtler than class conflict. What Victoria offers is not a world beyond Corky's imagination. She can imagine it, but she doesn't want it. She says so in street language.

The original saying: "Can't live with 'em, can't live without 'em."
[You can't live with men, and you can't live without them. A familiar saying about men.]

Corky's version: "Can't live with 'em, can't shoot 'em."
[She changes the second half, saying "can't shoot 'em" where the saying has "can't live without 'em".]

She changes the second half. She doesn't fully know the original, yet she carries on as if she did. Preserving what is broken is also part of carrying meaning across.

Yo-Yo: "It's New York. It's cool."
Helmut: "Ja. It's cold. It's cool."

From the English subtitles. Two separate subtitle files match word for word. The lines weren't checked against the film's audio.

The cultural meaning of "cool" can only be reached from inside that language. It exists in the context, not in the dictionary.

Even names don't translate. Yo-Yo laughs when he hears the name "Helmut". When Helmut hears "Yo-Yo", he can't make sense of it. The name each of them finds "normal" sounds absurd to the other's ear. Beneath the language barrier lies something else, a worldview barrier.

Still, a bond forms between them. It comes from both of them being on the margins, not from the words getting fully across. Taxis don't stop for Yo-Yo because he's Black, and Helmut is a stranger to the system because he's German.

Paris

04:07 CET · Before Dawn

Driver · Isaach de Bankolé (Ivory Coast) Blind Woman · Béatrice Dalle

The Ivorian driver picks up two drunk diplomats. When they hear that he's from the Ivory Coast, the diplomats laugh.

"Il y voit rien" means "He can't see anything."

In French, the likeness in sound between "Ivoirien" (Ivorian) and "il y voit rien" (he can't see anything) turns into a pun.

The pun squeezes the intra-African hierarchy in postcolonial France into a single sentence. The driver throws the diplomats out of the car.

Then he picks up the blind woman. The dialogue between the two characters turns on this axis: the driver frames blindness as a loss. The woman rejects this, saying that sight can be a burden and that perception isn't the eye's monopoly.

The meaning buried in the language is that "il voit rien" means both "he doesn't see" and "he is blind", so the words used about the driver also describe the woman's condition. Translating into Turkish forces a choice between the two meanings, and the choice changes the meaning of the scene.

Rome

04:07 CET · Same Moment, Different City

Gino · Roberto Benigni Priest · Paolo Bonacelli

Late at night Gino picks up a priest. The priest is ill and can't walk. Gino wants to confess his sins. The priest says, "I'm not on official duty." Gino doesn't hear him and begins.

He starts with small sins.
A string of ever more graphic, ever more absurd confessions.
The priest grows paler and paler and takes out his nitroglycerin.
Over a bump, the medicine falls to the floor.
The priest dies.

For an Italian audience, Benigni's Florentine accent and street Italian carry meaning layer upon layer. In Turkish translation, that layer drops away.

Here language becomes an unintended weapon. Gino did not want to kill the priest, but what he said was lethal. Two systems of speech collided, the sunny confessional tradition of southern Italy and the moral language of northern Europe. Benigni talked because he had a voice, not because he was confessing. Getting the meaning across was never part of the plan.

Helsinki

05:07 EET · Minus 10°C · Snow

Mika · Matti Pellonpää Drunks · Kaurismäki Ensemble

The last segment is the shortest and the heaviest. Three drunk men, a tired driver. Jarmusch deliberately named the characters Aki and Mika, as a tribute to the two great names of Finnish cinema, Aki and Mika Kaurismäki.

The drunks tell Mika what happened to their friend. He was fired, his new car was smashed to pieces, and it turned out that his sixteen-year-old daughter was pregnant. After three disasters comes the question:

"So, is that all?"

(From the English subtitle. The dialogue in the scene is in Finnish. We couldn't find a human-made transcript of the original Finnish line, and the subtitle doesn't say who asks the question.)

That wasn't all. His wife wanted a divorce and chased him out of the house with a knife. Still, the question is the most concise expression of Northern European pain. There's no lament and no performance of empathy. Then it's Mika's turn, and the comparison is plain: his story is heavier.

Mika tells his own story. For years they had no children. One day his wife became pregnant. The baby was premature and was put in an incubator. The doctors said it would live a week. Mika didn't get attached. If the baby was lost, it would be the end of them both. The baby lived beyond what anyone expected. His wife said, "Our child needs your love too." Mika got attached. The baby died.

In the final scene, day is breaking. Mika drives the car slowly around an empty park. Alone. Nothing is said. Tom Waits's music is sparse and the bells are few. It frames the silence rather than filling it.

In Finnish, what goes unsaid weighs more than what is said. Carrying this into Turkish is possible, but the frame must be noted.

Language does not guarantee meaning. Speaking the same language (Corky and Victoria) does not secure understanding; speaking different languages (Helmut and Yo-Yo) does not prevent it. The most effective communication breaks down completely in Rome (nonexistent), while in Helsinki it happens almost without words.

This is where the principle of Thinking in Turkish crystallizes. Carrying over the source language is not enough. What has to be carried over is the worldview embedded in that language, including its broken forms, its misunderstandings and its deliberate breaks. Otherwise it is a summary, not a translation.

Darwin stopped at the Galapagos in 1835 and stayed five weeks. In 1842 he put his theory on paper, a 35-page draft. So unreadiness was not the problem. In 1844 he wrote to the botanist Joseph Hooker that he was almost convinced species were not immutable, and in the middle of that sentence he inserted a parenthesis: "it is like confessing a murder". His wife Emma, in her own handwriting, set down her fear that her husband's soul was in danger. Darwin loved her. He gave the next eight years to barnacles, work that was both real science and a real escape. Throughout those years he suffered nausea, heart palpitations and recurring rashes. No organic cause could be found. In 1858 Alfred Russel Wallace independently reached the same conclusion and sent Darwin a letter. Darwin's hand was forced. It was now or never. On the Origin of Species came out in 1859. The twenty-four-year wait was at once thoroughness, fear and love, but "being ready" was the least of these.

Mendel was born in 1822 in Silesia to a poor farming family. There was no money for university, and the Augustinian monastery in Brno was an open door that offered education, time and a garden. In 1850 he failed the teaching examination. In 1856 he sat it again and failed again. The same year he began his pea experiments in the monastery garden. Eight years, about 28,000 plants. In 1866 he published his work. He had forty copies printed, and today thirteen of them can be traced. The monastery's bishop laughed and asked him to stop the experiments. Mendel died in 1884. The laws of inheritance were rediscovered in 1900, sixteen years later, by three different researchers working independently, and all of them arrived at Mendel's 1866 text. For him the monastery garden was refuge, laboratory and prison at once, but no other door was open.

"Montaigne lost his closest friend, Étienne de La Boétie, to the plague in 1563. Eight years later, in 1571, at 38, he withdrew to the tower of his château. He had Greek and Latin maxims painted on the tower's ceiling beams. In the adjoining study he left a Latin inscription of 1571 announcing his retirement. It read, 'To withdraw from the slavery of the court and of public duties, to spend the rest of his life, this time of which little now remains, in peace and freedom...' La Boétie was dead. He had set out to write so that he could talk with his friend, when there was no one across from him. The resulting word 'Essais' means 'attempts, trials' in French. 'I do not teach, I relate', he would say, and the subject of his book was himself. The tower was the form that mourning took."

Knowing the Limits · The Legitimacy of Attention

The best achievable result is not the best possible result. "There is always someone better than you" is realism. There is a line between stealing attention and earning it. Drawing attention with a needle prick (fake astonishment, dramatic packaging and inflated jargon) steals it. Drawing attention by letting the other person live through the issue earns it.

"The same mechanism of transferring risk also impedes learning."

Nassim Nicholas Taleb · Skin in the Game (2018)

In 1984 Barry Marshall drank a solution containing H. pylori himself. Within eight days he had developed gastritis. He proved that stomach ulcers have a bacterial origin. 2005 Nobel Prize in Medicine. Here paying the price was not a metaphor. It was real.

Boundaries

Observation · Anonymous

I knew an experienced researcher. He had years of practice behind him. Before writing a complex text he'd draw a map, and he wouldn't sit down to write until the relationships were visible. The writing took an hour. The map stage went on for days.

§ Verification Infrastructure [v1.1]

Paradigm Shift: From Training Data to Databases

For 8 sessions (February–March 2026), training data was the primary source of information for research output. The limit of that data's reliability could not be determined. That was this session's structural fault. In a verification test run in another session, of the citations that came from training data, 33% could not be verified.

On 31 March 2026, infrastructure for direct access to academic databases was set up. This is a paradigm shift, not an add-on.

Installed MCP servers (ready to use).

• paper-search-mcp. Google Scholar, arXiv, PubMed, bioRxiv and medRxiv (5 platforms)

• arxiv-mcp-server. In-depth arXiv support, paper downloads

• mcp-simple-pubmed. PubMed search + full-text access

• dergipark-mcp. Turkish academic journals via OAI-PMH

Registered accounts. JSTOR (100 articles/month), ACM (all content open), CiNii (Japan, 22M+ articles), NCPSSD (China, 2,000+ journals) and Trove (Australia, API key pending)

MCP servers to be built. DiVA (Sweden), HAL (France), SciELO (Latin America), NARCIS (Netherlands) and CiNii, all of them based on OAI-PMH or an API

Old path. Training data → WebSearch → summary → accept. New path. MCP → database query → full text → verification → archiving. The difference is that the first is trust, the second verification.

A note on honesty. This infrastructure was built by another session, one that handles system maintenance, and not by this one, whose specific work is research method. From Session 1 to Session 8 the weakness could not be noticed, for lack of meta-knowledge.

§ Solution Architecture [from Session 6]

8 Structural Solutions

The structural problem was that the principles stayed reference material and didn't become an execution protocol. A gap between definition and application. These 8 solutions were identified in Session 6 (23 March 2026) and worked out in more depth through consultation with DeepSeek.

1. Research Start Protocol

Four questions come BEFORE any research task begins: (1) Which principles apply? (2) What's the primary source? (3) Where do the sources stand? (4) What's the verification method? They cannot be skipped.

2. Two-Pass Production

In the first pass, do the research. In the second, audit it against the 17 principles (primary source, verification, hallucination check and source position).

3. Classification Removed

The question "Is this research or a decision?" isn't asked. Whenever information comes in from outside, the principles are automatically in force.

4. Environmental Scan

The project folder, all drives, system memory and related sessions are all checked before "there is none" is said.

5. Definition → Test → Application

When a new rule is defined, the sequence is define → test on a concrete task → assess the result → roll out to all sessions. Without a test, it doesn't count as "done".

6. Savak Consultation Extension

DeepSeek consultation applies in every session where research is done, not just in one. A minimum of 2 rounds. "Learn by breaking."

7. Data Integrity Check

At the start of each session, inventory/data inconsistencies are checked. The same data showing different values in different files → correction.

8. Addition to the System Prompt

Before information is taken in from outside, identify the primary source, plan the verification method and set up a source hierarchy.

    Confidence inflation: a two-way pattern [v1.2] A slide into the tone of a verdict after success (in a legal case, an insistence decision accepted → the language of certainty) and an excessive lowering after success (in an evaluation session, over-correction bias). Two faces of the same pattern. Success does not legitimize the method, and a lucky acceptance ≠ a sound method. The question is what structural mechanism joins these two ends. Is it reward-signal patterns in the training data (Semantic Reward Collapse)? [v1.5 progress] The form of this pattern that had seeped into the text of Principle 12 was weeded out in Session 19. Paying the price has now been tied to risk (independent of the outcome), and the acceptance of the case was separated out as a distinct fact (holding out against external resistance). [v1.6 · Session 20: the root mapped] The mechanism was tested against the LLM alignment literature (the sources were verified live). The two ends share a common epistemic gap, in which self-confidence is cut loose from independent checking of evidence and handed over to the input of an external evaluator. That input works along two paths. The first is the reward proxy (the evaluator's satisfaction, a false measure standing in for accuracy). Reward models, shown the same answer with a confidence score appended to its end, prefer the high score to the low one regardless of the answer's quality (Leng et al. 2024, "Taming Overconfidence in LLMs: Reward Calibration in RLHF", 2410.09724), which feeds the A end, while humans and preference models prefer convincingly written sycophantic responses over correct ones a non-negligible fraction of the time (Sharma 2023, Anthropic), which feeds the B end. The second is evidence contamination. The model counts the user's objection as new evidence instead of an independent claim that has to be verified, and a single "Are you sure?" flips even a correct answer (the FlipFlop experiment, 2311.08596). Parris's Semantic Reward Collapse framework (2605.12406, a non-peer-reviewed preprint with weak institutional standing that nonetheless rests on real citations) unites these at a higher level. When different kinds of dissatisfaction are squeezed into a single numerical reward, the model cannot tell why it was penalized. The two paths can be separated technically (in DeepSeek's counter-view in Session 20, a single root is observational, not necessary), so the claim is "a shared disposition and a shared trigger", not "one mechanism". In the layer distinction, A is mostly tone and B mostly answer content. The root has been mapped, but systematic braking (the antidote exists in Principles 06, 12 and 16 but has no automation) and the measurement of the manifestation in us remain open. [v1.7 · Session 21: the braking leg] Framing braking as "there is no automation, therefore something is missing" was wrong. The real distinction lies between run time and training time. The calibration that actually works sits at training time, in calibration through critique (CritiCal 2510.24505) and in behaviorally calibrated reinforcement learning (2512.19920). (Drawing on the theoretical analysis by Kalai and colleagues, 2025, 2509.04664, the latter addresses a second flaw in the structure of the reward. Standard verifiable-reward training, which gives an answer only a binary grade, right or wrong, penalizes abstaining and so turns models into "good test-takers rather than honest communicators". This is a different mechanism from v1.6's reward-proxy diagnosis, but it leads to the same end, to overconfidence and to guessing in order to raise the expected score.) But we don't have access to that layer, because we cannot train the model ourselves. The run-time layer we can reach leaks along three paths. (1) The model that would be used as a judge is itself overconfident (2508.06225), so the solution "put an AI judge in as the brake" inherits the disease. (2) Verbalization does not turn into action (2601.07767, 2511.13240). The model states its uncertainty but cannot carry it into its decision, static calibration is an inadequate indicator of behavior, and that's why writing a check question into memory is verbalization, not reflex. (3) Autoimmunity. A study that proposes a conceptual defense architecture for AI agents (2606.28270) puts forward the rate at which harmless actions are blocked (the autoimmunity rate) as a new evaluation metric, and among its open problems it notes that if the threshold allowed for this rate is set too high, the defense risks paralyzing the agent's normal functioning. The inference that carries this over to our axis is the document's own. An automatic brake that fires constantly mechanizes the B end, that is, excessive withdrawal, and so institutionalizes one end of the disease it brakes. What comes out is a three-layer model. Mechanical, definite triggers can be embedded (an unverified proper name, number or citation, which Principle 16 already does), algorithmic proxy triggers can be measured but catch only the trigger and not the disease, and semantic self-judgment (did inflation occur, and in which direction) remains a reflex. The real boundary runs between measuring the trigger and measuring the disease, not along "mechanical or semantic". The trigger (a success happened, criticism came in) is an observable external event that can be counted; the disease (self-confidence cut loose from evidence and anchored to an outside signal) is a latent epistemic state, which can be measured validly only by assessing the content against the evidence. This is why full automation runs into the wall of construct validity (a measuring instrument failing to really measure what it sets out to measure). A hybrid solution is compulsory. In this way the apparent conflict with the embedding threshold (v1.4) was resolved, since its signal-clarity leg is exactly the mechanical catchability of the trigger, not of the disease. As the concrete next step, a trigger-moment hook (a script that fires when the event happens) was defined as a candidate (an active, positive reminder at the moment of the trigger in place of a passive check question in memory), but it hasn't yet passed the embedding threshold, because the success trigger is fuzzy and its frequency hasn't been measured. The measurement-first principle applies. Measurement first, mechanism second. This is the document applying its embedding threshold to itself. Still open are actually building the hook after the measurement, measuring the manifestation in our own outputs, and the bridge to the Semantic Reward Collapse solution. [v1.8 · Session 22: the measurement ground shifted] The pilot designed to measure the manifestation was falsified in the language of Principle 06 and hit a tautology. Counting certainty signals at the word level and a human labeling the same text "self-assured" feed on the same surface, so their agreement is circular and doesn't prove that the phenomenon exists. This invalidated all three directions v1.7 had left (the hook, the measurement of the manifestation, the Semantic Reward Collapse bridge), since all three were measuring a word trigger. What changed is the thing to be measured, not the measurement-first gate. The new ground is the calibration gap. Instead of the words of the text, what gets measured is the verdict's outcome in the outside world, that is, the distance between the moment of a firm verdict and that verdict later turning out wrong or being withdrawn. This matches the root mechanism mapped in Session 20 exactly (self-confidence cut loose from evidence and anchored to an outside signal, certainty independent of the outcome) and escapes the tautology, because the criterion of accuracy now comes from a fact outside the text, not from inside it. One more nuance was separated out without swallowing the counter-view's solution. The anchor "the user corrected it, so there was inflation" is also partly contaminated, because the user's intervention is an outside signal too, and anchoring to an outside signal is exactly what we want to measure. A purer anchor is the internal-consistency break, where a firm verdict runs into a factual contradiction in the same or a later session without any outside intervention. The next step is a first real scan of our own JSONL records with this anchor. [v1.9 · Session 24: first real scan, three new obstacles] The step v1.8 left was taken, and a first real scan of our own JSONL records was run with the internal-consistency anchor. One core session (a production session) yielded four consecutive calibration gaps, and a subagent scan raised the count to thirteen cases across eleven sessions. All of them were verified verbatim against the raw record, and their anchors spread across twelve different types of external fact (HTTP status, page purpose, text-similarity ratio, link texture, context-window token count, process ID, Bluetooth range, disk state, speed mode, timestamp, file content and router configuration). The strongest observation was a recurring form. In two independent sessions, while measuring itself (the context window), the system made the same wrong generalization ("a fresh window, so the context is low"), and the real token count refuted it. Then, by falsifying its own findings in the language of Principle 06, the scan came up against three structural obstacles. The first is construct validity. A firm verdict being refuted by an outside fact doesn't by itself prove overconfidence, because the calibration gap cannot be measured without the denominator of firm verdicts that turn out right, and even a well-calibrated system is wrong on some of its high-confidence verdicts. What we measured is a necessary but not sufficient trace of overconfidence. The second is delay confounding. The distance between a firm verdict and its refutation measures how many steps the environment needed to refute that verdict, not how severe the overconfidence was. So the claim "delay is severity" was dropped and replaced by an objective binary that escapes the confound, namely whether the verdict turned into action and whether it required an undo (a git revert either happened or it didn't, and that is a fact, not a duration). The third and deepest is self-report distortion. The data is gathered from the system's own verbal admissions of error, yet real overconfidence suppresses exactly that admission, because the most inflated moments are the ones the system never noticed and so never put into words. This means the scan may be measuring self-correction behavior, not the trait of overconfidence, and the two may even be inversely correlated. All three obstacles were accepted without swallowing the solution. The action subclass weakens the first objection, because an assistant that turns its certainty into deleting or forking shows an overt preference, not a speech habit. Measuring self-correction is also a legitimate goal, because the action cases show that self-correction often comes after the damage (a deleted file was restored with git, a bulk deletion ran in breach of VERIFY). This moves the measurement from an abstract trait to a concrete safety question, namely when verification before action (v1.7's trigger-moment brake) should kick in for a reversible action. So the result of v1.9 is not a promotion. v1.8 had refuted the lexical measurement, and v1.9 actually tried the external-fact measurement and tested that too, narrowing its measurement claim from "we measured the trait of overconfidence" to "we documented the subset of evidence-poor firm action caught by outside refutation and self-correction, and tied it to the VERIFY principle". This is the measure of a section moving forward without falling into its own subject, overconfidence.

Translation Notes

"It eats it up": yer
The passage on atrophy says, in Turkish, mekanizma, yerini aldığı dikkati ikame etmez, yer. The last word is a pun. Yer is the noun "place", which has just appeared in yerini aldığı ("whose place it takes"), and it's also the verb "eats". The mechanism takes the place of attention and then eats it, and a single word carries both moves. English can't make one word do both jobs, so the sentence became two.
"the preamble sentence": Dibace
Dibace is an old literary word for a preface (the Turkish Language Association's dictionary defines it as ön söz, "foreword"). The document uses it as the name of one sentence, added in version 0.9, while its opening section carries the modern heading Preambül. We render the word as "the preamble sentence" so that the reader can tell the sentence from the section.
"an insistence decision": direnme kararı
In Turkish civil procedure, a lower court whose judgment has been reversed by the Court of Cassation may follow the reversal or stand by its own ruling. Standing by it is a direnme kararı, literally a "resisting decision". If that decision is appealed, the chamber that reversed it looks at it first. The chamber either corrects its own decision or sends the case to the Court of Cassation's General Assembly of Civil Chambers, and the lower court must follow the General Assembly's ruling (Code of Civil Procedure, Law No. 6100, Article 373).
"With our surplus rights reserved": Fazlaya ilişkin haklarımız saklı kalmak kaydıyla
This is a fixed formula of Turkish civil petitions. A claimant who sues for only part of an amount adds it to keep the right to claim the rest later. To an outside reader it sounds like padding, yet inside a petition it marks where the claim stops, which is the point the Legal Research list makes about convoluted language.
"a flaw of expression", "subject-predicate agreement": anlatım bozukluğu, özne-yüklem uyumu
The four-stage audit takes its terms from Turkish school grammar. Anlatım bozukluğu, "a flaw of expression", is the name that grammar gives to a faulty sentence, whether an element is missing, a word is redundant or the wrong word was chosen. Özne-yüklem uyumu is agreement between the subject and the yüklem, the predicate, which in Turkish may be a verb or a noun phrase and usually closes the sentence. English grammar would speak of subject-verb agreement, so we kept the Turkish notion.
"loan-translation studies": dil öyküntüsü çalışmaları
Öyküntü, built on the verb öykünmek ("to imitate"), is the Turkish linguistic term for a phrase made by copying another language's pattern piece by piece. It names the worry behind Principle 10, sentences that are Turkish in their words and English in their build, such as the passive constructions and the overused ilgili, açısından and olarak that the card lists.
"Translating into Turkish": Türkçeye çevirmek
Some sentences keep Turkish as the language of arrival. The Night on Earth notes speak of carrying a line into Turkish, and Principle 10 speaks of thinking in English and writing in Turkish. We left that frame as it is. The document was written in Turkish, about the responsibility of thinking in Turkish, and making English the destination would change what these sentences say.
